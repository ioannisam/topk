\chapter{Results and evaluation}
\label{ch:evaluation}

This chapter presents the results and interprets them in terms of the
mechanisms of the three preceding chapters. Unless stated otherwise, the plots
concern 32-bit integers and a uniform input distribution. The effect of data
type and distribution is examined separately in sections~\ref{sec:eval-dtypes}
and~\ref{sec:eval-distributions}.

\section{Correctness}
\label{sec:eval-correctness}

Every timing comparison presupposes a correct result. The main sweep covers
2700 cases: three architectures, three algorithms, five data types, input sizes
from $2^3$ to $2^{24}$, four values of k. All were verified against the
verification implementation (section~\ref{sec:meth-correctness}) with a 100\%
pass rate. Every time that follows therefore corresponds to a correct set of k
elements.

\section{Execution time}
\label{sec:eval-time}

    \label{fig:eval-time-n-k8}
\end{figure}

In the algorithmic scope (left), the graphics unit is faster at the smallest
input, \qty{0.010}{\milli\second} against \qty{0.022}{\milli\second} for the
processor, and again at the largest, \qty{0.43}{\milli\second} against
\qty{1.23}{\milli\second}. Across almost all of the range in between, however,
the processor leads. The exact crossover point moves between repeated runs of
the same sweep. At such small absolute times both architectures are close to
the timer's resolution, so there the ranking more likely reflects measurement
noise.

The neural processing unit shows a clear floor at small sizes. The curves are
flat at around half a millisecond up to about $2^{16}$ elements, dominated by
the fixed cost of dispatch and waiting. The processor has an analogous but much
lower floor, on the order of five hundredths of a millisecond.

Only one point stands out. At the smallest size, where n and k coincide,
map-reduce on the neural processing unit drops noticeably below this floor.
This is due to the sampling of section~\ref{sec:npu-mapreduce}. When n=k=8, the
sample that seeds the heap covers the entire input and no job ever reaches the
tiles.

In the end-to-end scope (right), with data transfer inside the measurement, the
picture is completely overturned. The processor is faster than every other
architecture at \emph{all} sizes, even the largest. The
\qty{0.43}{\milli\second} of the graphics unit's kernel becomes
\qty{5.69}{\milli\second} in total, while the processor stays at
\qty{1.23}{\milli\second}, since for it the two scopes coincide.

The algorithmic scope shows how well each device computes; the total answers
whether it is worth assigning the workload to it. The neural processing unit
needs \qty{9.97}{\milli\second} at the largest input, more than any other
architecture in both scopes.

Comparing the same bitonic sort on the three architectures is illuminating,
since it executes the same layer schedule and the difference is due solely to
the implementation. The two general-purpose architectures trade places along
the range: the processor is faster over most of the small to medium sizes, the
graphics unit only at the largest, reaching a factor of six at the largest
size, consistent with the difference in bandwidth. As above, the exact
crossover point moves between runs and should not be read as a fixed threshold.
The neural processing unit stays close to the processor at these larger sizes,
consistent with its time being spent almost entirely on the host.

Map-reduce does not admit the same comparison in the first place, since each
architecture maintains and merges its local heaps in a fundamentally different
way (chapters~\ref{ch:cpu} to~\ref{ch:npu}). The right-hand figure shows
instead how differently the same algorithmic idea unfolds in three
implementations. The graphics unit is the fastest across almost the whole
range, with the processor following at a roughly constant distance, except at
small sizes, where the same caveat about measurement noise applies. At the
largest input the graphics unit stays faster, but without the intermediate
crossover of bitonic sort. The neural processing unit here does not approach
the processor (in sharp contrast to its behaviour in bitonic sort) and stays
about an order of magnitude slower than both at every size.

The dependence on k is where the ranking of the algorithms is overturned. For
bitonic sort the four k curves nearly coincide, since k only affects the size
of the sorted blocks. For map-reduce on the graphics unit the same curves are
three orders of magnitude apart.

The fastest implementation for small k becomes the slowest for large k, a
difference of three orders of magnitude. Map-reduce on the graphics unit slows
down by a factor of 2800 between the two extremes; bitonic sort on the same
architecture by barely six. At small k the processor's two implementations rank
higher than at large k: map-reduce overtakes bitonic sort on the graphics unit,
and bitonic sort overtakes that of the neural processing unit. The overturn,
however, stays fixed, and the cheapest implementation for k=8 is never the
cheapest for k=131072.

Independent studies on graphics units from two different manufacturers record
the same dependence on k, and they too address it with adaptive algorithm
switching~\cite{zhang2023gputopk,lin2026adaptivetopk}. It is therefore rather
unlikely to be a peculiarity of this particular implementation. There, the
growth in time of partial-sorting methods with k is attributed explicitly to
the $O(\log^2 k)$ complexity of the underlying bitonic network.

The cause was given in section~\ref{sec:gpu-mapreduce-limit}. The heap filters
only once it is full, and the number of threads that contribute is capped at
the ratio $n/k$. For the sizes of table~\ref{tab:eval-k-inversion} this ratio
is 128. In other words, the device effectively employs one hundred and
twenty-eight threads out of the thousands it has. The processor, with only
eight threads, never runs into the same limit, so its slowdown is markedly
milder.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{int/time/time_vs_n_algo_compare_gpu_k131072}
    \caption{The three algorithms on the graphics unit for k equal to 131072.}
    \label{fig:eval-algo-compare-gpu}
\end{figure}

There is no universally preferable algorithm. The choice between a sorting
network and heap-based selection depends on the ratio $n/k$, not on the
architecture.

Comparing each implementation with the baseline of the same architecture
neutralises hardware differences, and so gives the most honest picture.

On the processor, map-reduce is faster than the library by a factor of three
for small k, although the speed-up is not constant. It peaks at thirteen times
for n equal to $2^{22}$, where the working set (16\,MB) fits exactly in the
processor's L3 cache (figure~\ref{fig:eval-cache-ladder}), and falls back again
once the input exceeds this limit. The limitation of
section~\ref{sec:cpu-reference} still applies, though: the library is
single-threaded, so the factor is not purely algorithmic.

A second notable speed-up appears elsewhere in the figure, a consequence of the
overturn of section~\ref{sec:eval-time-k}. At large k, where heap-based
selection collapses, bitonic sort on the processor becomes about six times
faster than the library, which also relies on a heap of size k and suffers the
same penalty, while the sorting network stays largely unaffected. The advantage
fades as the input grows further, the network's data traffic starts to
dominate, and it narrows to a little above one at the largest input.

On the graphics unit the comparison is made on completely equal terms, given
that the same device and the same level of parallelism are used. Map-reduce
wins for k=8, while for large k the library is faster by more than a thousand
times, since radix selection has no collapse point with respect to k
(section~\ref{sec:gpu-reference-cub}).

On the neural processing unit, for the integer types the figure refers to, no
implementation surpasses the library anywhere, since the same processor that
would run the library is consumed in any case by preparing data for the unit
(section~\ref{sec:npu-bottlenecks}). The qualification about integer types is
deliberate. For floating point, especially half precision, the picture is
overturned and the neural processing unit does surpass the library there. The
explanation is given in section~\ref{sec:eval-dtypes}.

\label{sec:eval-roofline}

    \centering

        \caption{k equal to 8.}
        \label{fig:eval-roofline-k8}
    \end{subfigure}

    \vspace{0.3em}

    \begin{subfigure}{\textwidth}
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=0.86\textwidth]{int/memory/roofline_kernels_k131072}
        \caption{k equal to 131072.}
        \label{fig:eval-roofline-k131072}
    \end{subfigure}
    \caption[Placement of the runs against the measured ceilings]{Placement of
    the runs against the measured ceilings, for the largest input. Restricted
    to the maximum size, where the working set exceeds the cache and the
    main-memory ceiling is the relevant one.}
    \label{fig:eval-roofline}
\end{figure}

Each run is placed here on a plane of operational intensity and achieved rate.
All implementations cluster on the left, at very low operational intensity, and
none approaches the horizontal part of the roof. No implementation, on any
architecture, is limited by compute capability. The finding agrees with an
independent measurement on data-centre accelerators, where the fastest known
selection kernel uses over ninety percent of the bandwidth against about half
of the compute capability, and the time scales with the bandwidth of each
device~\cite{zhang2023gputopk}.

Juxtaposing the two values of k highlights how sharply the algorithms differ in
their use of the hardware. On the one hand, bitonic sort on the graphics unit
sits consistently on the sloped part of the theoretical roof, regardless of k.
This position visually confirms the finding of
section~\ref{sec:gpu-bottlenecks}. The implementation uses the available
bandwidth almost fully and is limited solely by the volume of data it has to
move.

On the other hand, the behaviour of map-reduce on the same architecture changes
entirely. While for k=8 it lies near the other points, at the large value of k
it collapses by three orders of magnitude, now far from both ceilings. This
placement (neither on the sloped nor on the horizontal part of the plot) is the
signature of a limitation independent of memory or compute. It reflects the
collapse of parallelism of section~\ref{sec:gpu-mapreduce-limit}.

This finding stems from the very nature of the problem. Because top-k selection
performs practically only one comparison per element, without reuse, its
operational intensity is low by definition.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{machine/cache_ladder}
    \caption{Measured read bandwidth as a function of working-set size, for
    the three architectures.}
    \label{fig:eval-cache-ladder}
\end{figure}

The maximum theoretical limit (ceiling) against which each run is compared
varies dynamically, since it is a function of the working set in each case
(section~\ref{sec:meth-roofline}). Figure~\ref{fig:eval-cache-ladder} shows the
measured scale. The distinct levels correspond to the tiers of the memory
hierarchy, while the drop at the right-hand edge marks the point where the
working set exceeds the capacity of the last tier.

\section{Energy assessment}
\label{sec:eval-energy}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{int/time/time_vs_n_metric_compare_gpu_k8}
    \caption[Algorithmic and end-to-end time on the graphics
    unit]{Algorithmic and end-to-end time on the graphics unit, for k equal
    to 8. The distance between the two curves of each algorithm is the cost of
    transfers and kernel launches.}
    \label{fig:eval-metric-compare}
\end{figure}

Before the energy measurements, figure~\ref{fig:eval-metric-compare} shows why
the two scopes diverge so much on the graphics unit. The end-to-end curve stays
steady near three tenths of a millisecond up to about $2^{16}$ elements, where
the kernel is essentially invisible and the time is spent on transfers and
launches. Even at the largest input, the end-to-end time remains a multiple of
the algorithmic time, about five times for bitonic sort and thirteen for
map-reduce.

The energy picture differs substantially from that of the times, because of the
symmetric measurement scope (section~\ref{sec:meth-energy-scope}). Each
architecture is charged the total cost of solving the problem.

At the largest input and small k, map-reduce on the processor consumes about
\qty{19}{\milli\joule} per run, the most economical implementation. The
corresponding ones on the graphics unit consume from \qty{108}{\milli\joule} to
\qty{128}{\milli\joule}, up to seven times more, despite being faster in the
algorithmic scope. The most expensive implementation of all is bitonic sort on
the graphics unit. The corresponding one on the processor costs
\qty{118}{\milli\joule}.

    \centering
    \begin{subfigure}{0.49\textwidth}
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{int/energy/energy_by_backend_net_k8}
        \caption{Net energy.}

        \centering

        \label{fig:eval-power}

    \caption[Net energy and power by architecture and algorithm]{Net energy

    \label{fig:eval-energy-power}
\end{figure}

Taken together, figures~\ref{fig:eval-energy} and~\ref{fig:eval-power} bring
out a distinction that is easily misread, especially for the neural processing
unit. It has the lowest power draw of all implementations, with its map-reduce
at \qty{7.9}{\watt} against \qty{15.6}{\watt} for the corresponding one on the
processor and \qty{18.6}{\watt} for the graphics unit. It consumes, however,
more than four times the energy of map-reduce on the processor,
\qty{0.080}{\joule} against \qty{0.019}{\joule}. Energy is the product of power
and time, and the unit needs about eight times the time for the same work. The
forty-nine-percent advantage in power is thus wiped out by the disadvantage of
the eightfold duration. The principle that moving data costs more energy than
the computation itself~\cite{chen2016eyeriss} holds here too, but the reuse
that would amortise it is missing. A low-power architecture pays off
energetically only if it finishes in comparable time, and that does not happen
here.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{int/energy/time_vs_energy}
    \caption[End-to-end time and energy per measured case]{End-to-end time and
    total energy of each measured case. Both quantities are minimised, so the
    preferable cases lie toward the bottom-left corner.}
    \label{fig:eval-time-energy}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:eval-time-energy} places each case on a plane of two criteria
minimised simultaneously. A case is dominated by another when the latter is no
worse in either of the two and better in at least one, so there is no reason
ever to choose it. The cases dominated by no other form the Pareto front.
There, improving one criterion requires giving ground on the other.

The result is illuminating precisely because it is negative. The measurements
form a narrow diagonal band, with a correlation coefficient of 0.97 between the
logarithms of time and energy. Of the 209 cases only two belong to the front,
both the same implementation, map-reduce on the processor at two neighbouring
sizes. There is no substantial trade-off between speed and energy. The fastest
configuration is also the most economical.

The reason follows from the two preceding figures. Power varies between
implementations by barely a factor of 2.3, while time varies by four orders of
magnitude. When one factor of a product is nearly constant, the product follows
the other. Trading longer duration for lower power, a genuine design lever in
other systems, simply does not exist here.

All cases on the front run on the processor. No configuration of the graphics
unit or the neural processing unit is Pareto-optimal anywhere in the parameter
space.

\label{sec:eval-dtypes}

        \midrule

        CPU map-reduce & 0.39 & 1.23 & 1.22 & 1.22 & 2.35 \\
        CPU reference & 13.27 & 4.65 & 4.63 & 5.50 & 5.54 \\
        GPU bitonic & 0.88 & 1.34 & 1.37 & 1.34 & 2.89 \\
        NPU bitonic & 7.71 & 8.17 & 8.37 & 8.23 & 25.38 \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    \caption[Algorithmic time by data type]{Algorithmic time by data type, in
    ms, for $2^{24}$ elements and k equal to 8.}
    \label{tab:eval-dtypes}
\end{table}

The implementations of this work track the width of the type, as expected of
memory-bound algorithms. Half precision is consistently the fastest; bitonic
sort on the processor completes in a little over half the time of single
precision, precisely because it moves half the bytes at the same rate of
comparisons.

The reference implementation behaves the opposite way, with half precision
recording the lowest performance. The difference reveals that the library is
limited primarily by the cost of the scalar comparison (which for half
precision includes a conversion), rather than by memory. The ranking among
types is therefore a characteristic of the implementation, independent of the
inherent limitations of the hardware.

Double precision on the neural processing unit stands out, at three times the
time of any other type. The overhead is due entirely to the additional
correction pass of section~\ref{sec:npu-refinement}, necessary because the
32-bit key is not enough for 64-bit values.

The combination of the two opposite behaviours produces the only point in the
whole work where the neural processing unit outperforms its library. For half
precision, bitonic sort on the unit is faster than the library in twenty-nine
of the measured cases, with the largest gap reaching a factor of two and a
half. The phenomenon appears more weakly in the other two floating-point types,
and not at all in the integer ones.

The recorded advantage stems more from the library's weakness in handling half
precision than from any inherent superiority of the unit. The array's kernel
works on 32-bit integers regardless of type, shifting the conversion cost to
the vectorised encoding stage on the host (section~\ref{sec:npu-encoding}). The
library, in contrast, performs scalar comparisons with an intermediate
conversion. Offloading pays off here because the established solution falls
short with this data type, but this does not mean the unit is stronger in
general.

\section{Sensitivity to the input distribution}
\label{sec:eval-distributions}

Through normalisation against the uniform distribution, the nine combinations
can be compared on the same plot, even though their absolute execution times
differ by orders of magnitude. In the figure below, an implementation
unaffected by the data appears as five equal bars of unit height. A sensitive
one diverges in both directions.

Bitonic sort, on both the processor and the graphics unit, stays practically
unaffected by the data distribution for both values of k, with a maximum
variation of about 9\%. This stability makes it an ideal control indicator. Any
substantial change in its execution time would be due solely to factors
external to the measurement and not to the inherent behaviour of the algorithm.
Bitonic sort on the neural processing unit also stands out here. Unlike the
other two architectures it \emph{is} sensitive to the distribution, with a
variation factor of fifty-three. The sensitivity does not come from the array,
which executes a fixed network, but from the heap-based merge the host
performs, which dominates the time (section~\ref{sec:npu-pipeline}).

At the opposite end, the heap-based implementations show considerable
sensitivity to the distribution, regardless of who wrote the code. The
reference library varies by a factor of seventy-two between ascending and
descending input. Our own map-reduce on the processor, by a factor of
twenty-five, the same mechanism as in section~\ref{sec:cpu-mapreduce}.
Sensitivity to the data is a property of the algorithm and is unavoidable for
any selection method based on a heap.

The graphics unit's reference library shows a third, distinct pattern. It slows
down by nearly the same factor in both directions, 4.95 for ascending and 4.92
for descending, without favouring one as the heaps do. Sorting does not change
the distribution of the values (section~\ref{sec:meth-distributions}), so this
is not the radix-adversarial digit distribution (section~\ref{sec:bg-radix}).
Instead it is a matter of position. The candidates that survive the critical
bucket end up clustered at one end of the array or the other, which
symmetrically burdens the library's parallel compaction step regardless of
which end.

Juxtaposing the two values of k isolates the cause of the sensitivity of
map-reduce on the graphics unit. While the implementation is highly sensitive
for k=8 (sorted input slows it down by a factor of 4.0), it stays nearly
unaffected for k=131072 (the same input changes it by only 4\%). The same code,
with the same data, changes behaviour because the limiting factor changes
there. At large k, performance is now determined by the lack of parallelism
(section~\ref{sec:gpu-mapreduce-limit}) and not by the rejection rate.
Naturally, the data distribution stops mattering.
