\chapter{Results and evaluation}
\label{ch:evaluation}

This chapter presents the measurement results and interprets them in
terms of the mechanisms described in the three preceding chapters.
Unless stated otherwise, the plots concern 32-bit integers and a uniform
input distribution. The effect of data type and distribution is examined
separately in sections~\ref{sec:eval-dtypes}
and~\ref{sec:eval-distributions}.

\section{Correctness}
\label{sec:eval-correctness}

A precondition for any timing comparison is the correctness of the
result. The main sweep comprises 2700 cases, covering all three
architectures, the three algorithms, the five data types, input sizes
from $2^3$ to $2^{24}$, and the four values of $k$. All were verified
against the ground truth of section~\ref{sec:meth-correctness}, and all
passed: a 100\% pass rate for every architecture and algorithm, with no
exceptions.

Every time reported in what follows therefore corresponds to an
implementation that produced the correct set of $k$ elements, and the
comparisons that follow genuinely concern the same computation.

In the left panel, which concerns the algorithmic scope, the graphics
unit is fastest at the smallest input, completing in
\qty{0.010}{\milli\second} against \qty{0.022}{\milli\second} for the
processor, and again at the largest, in \qty{0.43}{\milli\second}
against \qty{1.23}{\milli\second}. For nearly the entire range in
between, however, the processor is the faster of the two, although the
exact size at which the two swap places is itself unstable across
repeated runs of the same sweep: at these small absolute times, both
architectures sit close enough to the timer's resolution that the
ranking there reflects measurement noise rather than a fixed
architectural difference.

The neural processing unit shows a clear floor at small sizes. Its
curves remain completely flat around half a millisecond up to about
$2^{16}$ elements: throughout this range the time does not depend on
input size at all, since it is dominated by the fixed cost of dispatch
and waiting. The processor shows an analogous but much lower floor, on
the order of five hundredths of a millisecond.

One point, though, stands out from this picture: at the smallest input
size, where $n$ and $k$ coincide, map-reduce on the neural processing
unit shows a time noticeably below the floor just described. The reason
lies in the sampling described in section~\ref{sec:npu-mapreduce}: when
$n$=$k$=8, the sample that seeds the heap covers the entire input, so no
work is ever dispatched to the tiles.

This picture is completely overturned in the right panel, though, once
the measurement includes data transfer as well. In the end-to-end scope
the processor is faster than every other architecture at \emph{all}
sizes examined, including the largest: the graphics unit kernel's
\qty{0.43}{\milli\second} becomes \qty{5.69}{\milli\second} in total,
while the processor stays at \qty{1.23}{\milli\second}, since for it the
two scopes coincide.

The algorithmic scope shows how well each device performs the
computation; the total answers whether it is worth delegating to it in
the first place. The neural processing unit requires
\qty{9.97}{\milli\second} at the largest input, more than any other
architecture under either scope.

Comparing the same bitonic sort across the three architectures is
illuminating, since the same level schedule is executed and the
difference is due solely to the implementation. The two general-purpose
architectures trade places along the range: the processor is faster than
the graphics unit through most of the small-to-medium sizes, and the
graphics unit pulls ahead only at the largest sizes, reaching a factor
of six at the largest size examined, consistent with the difference in
available bandwidth. As with the comparison above, the exact size at
which the two swap places moves between repeated runs of the same sweep
and should not be read as a fixed threshold. The neural processing unit
sits close to the processor at these larger sizes, consistent with the
finding that its time is spent almost entirely on the host.

Map-reduce admits no such comparison in principle, since each
architecture maintains and merges its local heaps in a fundamentally
different way (chapters~\ref{ch:cpu} through~\ref{ch:npu}); the right
panel instead shows how differently the same algorithmic idea plays out
once implemented three times over. The graphics unit is the fastest of
the three across almost the entire range, with the processor trailing at
a roughly constant distance except at small sizes, where the same caveat
about measurement noise applies as above. At the largest input the
graphics unit remains the faster of the two, as above, but without the
intermediate crossover bitonic sort shows. In sharp contrast to its
bitonic behaviour, the neural processing unit is not close to the
processor here: it stays roughly an order of magnitude slower than both
architectures at every size, reaching its already-noted time at the
largest input.

The dependence on $k$ is the point at which the ranking of the
algorithms is overturned. The juxtaposition in
figure~\ref{fig:eval-k-colored} captures the phenomenon directly.
For bitonic sort the curves of the four values of $k$ nearly
coincide, since $k$ affects only the size of the sorted blocks.
For map-reduce on the graphics unit the same curves are separated
by three orders of magnitude.

The fastest implementation for small $k$ becomes the slowest for large
$k$, a difference of three orders of magnitude. Map-reduce on the
graphics unit slows down by a factor of 2800 between the two extremes,
while bitonic sort on the same architecture slows down by barely a
factor of six. At small $k$ the processor's two implementations also
rank higher than at large $k$ -- map-reduce overtaking bitonic sort on
the graphics unit, and bitonic sort overtaking that on the neural
processing unit -- but the overturn itself is unaffected: the
implementation cheapest at $k$ equal to 8 is never the implementation
cheapest at $k$ equal to 131072.

This overturn is not a peculiarity of the present implementation.
Independent studies on graphics units from two different manufacturers
report the same dependence on $k$ and address it with adaptive algorithm
switching~\cite{zhang2023gputopk,lin2026adaptivetopk}; there, the growth
in time of partial-sorting methods with $k$ is attributed explicitly to
the $O(\log^2 k)$ complexity of the underlying bitonic network.

The cause was given in section~\ref{sec:gpu-mapreduce-limit}: the heap
filters only once it is full, so the number of threads that contribute
is capped at the ratio $n/k$. For the sizes in
table~\ref{tab:eval-k-inversion} this ratio is 128, i.e., the device
effectively employs one hundred and twenty-eight threads out of the
thousands it has available. The processor, with only eight threads,
never runs into this same limit, which is why its slowdown is markedly
milder.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{int/time/time_vs_n_algo_compare_gpu_k131072}
    \caption{The three algorithms on the graphics unit for k equal to 131072.}
    \label{fig:eval-algo-compare-gpu}
\end{figure}

The practical conclusion is that no algorithm is universally preferable.
The choice between a sorting network and heap-based selection depends on
the ratio $n/k$, not on the architecture.

Comparing each implementation with the reference of the same
architecture gives the most honest picture, since it neutralises
hardware differences.

On the processor, map-reduce is faster than the library by a factor of
three for small $k$, although the speedup is not constant. It peaks at
thirteen times for $n$ equal to $2^{22}$, where the working set (16 MB)
fits exactly in the processor's L3 cache
(figure~\ref{fig:eval-cache-ladder}), and falls back again once the
input exceeds this limit. It should be remembered, though, that the
limitation of section~\ref{sec:cpu-reference} applies here: the library
is single-threaded, so this factor is not purely algorithmic.

The largest speed-up in the whole figure appears elsewhere, and is a
consequence of the overturn in section~\ref{sec:eval-time-k}. For large
$k$, where heap-based selection collapses, bitonic sort on the processor
reaches roughly six times faster than the library. The reason is that
the library, too, relies on a heap of size $k$ and suffers the same
overhead, while the sorting network stays essentially unaffected. The
advantage weakens as the input grows further and the network's data
traffic starts to dominate, narrowing to just above unity at the largest
input.

On the graphics unit the comparison is strict, since the library runs on
the same device with the same parallelism. There, map-reduce outperforms
for $k$ equal to 8, while for large $k$ the library is faster by more than
a thousand times. Radix selection has no point of collapse with respect to
$k$, as explained in section~\ref{sec:gpu-reference-cub}.

On the neural processing unit, for the integer types the figure covers,
no implementation surpasses the library in any parameter region, since
the very processor that would run the library is in any case consumed
by preparing the data for the device (section~\ref{sec:npu-bottlenecks}).

The qualifier ``for the integer types'' is not a hedge. For
floating-point types, and especially for half precision, the picture
overturns and the neural processing unit does surpass the library; the
explanation is given in section~\ref{sec:eval-dtypes}, after the
behaviour across types is first presented.

    \label{fig:eval-roofline}
\end{figure}

The diagram places each run at its level of arithmetic intensity and
achieved throughput. All implementations cluster in the left-hand
region, i.e., at very low arithmetic intensity, and none approaches the
horizontal part of the ceiling. No implementation of this work, on any
architecture, is limited by compute capability. This finding agrees with
an independent measurement on data-centre accelerators, where the
fastest known selection kernel utilises over ninety percent of the
available bandwidth against about half of compute capability, and where
time scales with each device's bandwidth~\cite{zhang2023gputopk}.

Juxtaposing the two values of $k$ adds two observations. In both cases
bitonic sort on the graphics unit sits practically on the sloped section
of its own ceiling, which visually confirms the finding of
section~\ref{sec:gpu-bottlenecks}: the implementation utilises the
available bandwidth almost fully and is limited exclusively by the
volume it is obliged to move.

The second observation concerns map-reduce on the same architecture. For
$k$ equal to 8 it sits near the other points, while for the large value
it collapses by three orders of magnitude and moves away from both
ceilings equally. This position, far from both the sloped and the
horizontal section, is the signature of a limitation that is neither
memory nor compute, and corresponds to the collapse of parallelism
described in section~\ref{sec:gpu-mapreduce-limit}.

This finding is structural rather than incidental. Top-k selection
performs essentially one comparison per element and reuses almost
nothing, so its arithmetic intensity is low by definition.

The ceiling against which each run is compared is not fixed but a
function of its working set, per section~\ref{sec:meth-roofline}.

\section{Energy assessment}
\label{sec:eval-energy}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{int/time/time_vs_n_metric_compare_gpu_k8}
    \caption[Algorithmic and end-to-end time on the graphics unit]{Algorithmic and end-to-end time on the graphics unit, for k equal to 8. The distance between the
    two curves of each algorithm is the cost of transfers and kernel launches.}
    \label{fig:eval-metric-compare}
\end{figure}

Before presenting the energy measurements,
figure~\ref{fig:eval-metric-compare} is useful, since it shows why the
two measurement scopes diverge so sharply on the graphics unit. The
end-to-end curve stays steady near three tenths of a millisecond up to
about $2^{16}$ elements: throughout this range the kernel is essentially
invisible and the time is spent entirely on transfers and launches. Even
at the largest input, the end-to-end time remains a multiple of the
algorithmic time, from about five times for bitonic sort to thirteen
times for map-reduce.

The energy picture differs substantially from the time picture, and the
difference is due to the symmetric measurement scope of
section~\ref{sec:meth-energy-scope}: each architecture is charged the
total cost of solving the problem, including the host.

At the largest input and for small $k$, map-reduce on the processor
consumes about \qty{19}{\milli\joule} per run and is the most economical
implementation. The corresponding implementations on the graphics unit
consume from \qty{108}{\milli\joule} to \qty{128}{\milli\joule}, i.e.,
up to seven times more, despite being faster in the algorithmic scope.
The most expensive implementation of all is bitonic sort on the graphics
unit, while the corresponding implementation on the processor costs
\qty{118}{\milli\joule}.

    \label{fig:eval-energy-power}
\end{figure}

Juxtaposing figures~\ref{fig:eval-energy} and~\ref{fig:eval-power}
brings out a distinction that is easy to conflate, and one that
particularly concerns the neural processing unit. This device has the
\emph{lowest} power draw of all implementations, with map-reduce
measuring \qty{7.9}{\watt} against \qty{15.6}{\watt} for the
corresponding implementation on the processor and \qty{18.6}{\watt} for
the graphics unit.

Nonetheless it consumes over four times the energy of map-reduce on the
processor, \qty{0.080}{\joule} against \qty{0.019}{\joule}. The reason
is that energy is the product of power and time, and the device needs
roughly eight times the time for the same task: the forty-nine-percent
advantage in power is wiped out by the eightfold disadvantage in
duration. The principle that moving data costs more energy than the computation itself
does~\cite{chen2016eyeriss} holds here too; what is missing is the reuse
that would amortise it. A low-power architecture pays off energetically
only if it completes the task in comparable time, and for this
particular problem that is not the case.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{int/energy/time_vs_energy}
    \caption[End-to-end time and energy per measured case]{End-to-end time and total energy of each measured case. Both
    quantities are minimised, so the preferable cases lie toward the
    bottom-left corner.}
    \label{fig:eval-time-energy}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:eval-time-energy} places each measured case in a plane
of two criteria minimised simultaneously. A case is said to be dominated
by another when the latter is no worse in either quantity and is better
in at least one; such a case is never worth choosing. The set of cases
dominated by none other constitutes the Pareto front, i.e., exactly
those choices where improving one criterion requires giving up ground on
the other.

The result is illuminating precisely because it is negative. The
measurements form a narrow diagonal band, with a correlation coefficient
between the logarithms of time and energy of 0.97. Of the 209 cases,
only two belong to the front, and both are the same implementation,
map-reduce on the processor, at two neighbouring input sizes. No
substantial trade-off exists between speed and energy for this problem,
in other words: the fastest configuration is also the most economical.

The reason follows from the two preceding figures. Power varies among
implementations by barely a factor of 2.3, while time varies by four
orders of magnitude. When one factor of a product is nearly constant,
the product follows the other. The possibility of trading longer
duration for lower power, a genuine design lever on other systems, does
not exist here.

The cases that make up the front all run on the processor. No
configuration of the graphics unit or the neural processing unit is
Pareto-optimal at any point of the parameter space examined.

This work's implementations track the width of the type, as expected of
memory-bound algorithms. Half precision is consistently the fastest,
with bitonic sort on the processor completing in just over half the time
of single precision, precisely because it moves half the bytes at the
same rate of comparisons.

The reference implementation behaves oppositely: half precision is its
slowest case. The difference reveals that the library is limited mainly
by the cost of the scalar comparison (which for half precision includes
a conversion), rather than by memory. The ranking among types is
therefore a characteristic of the implementation, not an inherent
property of the hardware.

Double precision on the neural processing unit stands out, at three
times the time of any other type. The overhead is due entirely to the
additional refinement pass of section~\ref{sec:npu-refinement}, required
because the 32-bit key is not enough to express 64-bit values.

The combination of these two opposite behaviours produces the single
point in the entire work where the neural processing unit outperforms
its own library. For half precision, bitonic sort on the device is
faster than the library in twenty-nine of the measured cases, with the
largest gap reaching a factor of two and a half. The phenomenon appears
more weakly in the other two floating-point types, and never appears for
the integer types.

The cause is not that the device handles half precision well, but that
the library handles it poorly. The array's kernel operates on 32-bit
integers regardless of type, so the cost of the type shifts to host
encoding, which for half precision is vectorised per
section~\ref{sec:npu-encoding}. The library, by contrast, performs
scalar comparisons with an intermediate conversion. Offloading proves
advantageous exactly where the established solution is weakest, in other
words, not where the device is strongest.

\section{Sensitivity to the input distribution}
\label{sec:eval-distributions}

Normalising against the uniform distribution lets all nine combinations
be compared on the same figure, even though their absolute times differ
by orders of magnitude. An implementation unaffected by the data appears
as five equal bars of height one, while a sensitive one appears with
bars diverging in both directions.

The figure below first confirms the expected: bitonic sort on the processor
and the graphics unit is practically unaffected by the distribution at
both values of $k$, with overall variation below a factor of 1.09. This
property makes it a reliable witness: if its time were to change, the
change would be attributable to the measurement, not the algorithm.

Heap-based implementations show the opposite extreme, and indeed
regardless of who wrote the code. The reference library varies by a
factor of seventy-two between ascending and descending input, but so
does our own map-reduce on the processor, by a factor of twenty-five,
through the same mechanism described in section~\ref{sec:cpu-mapreduce}.
Sensitivity to the data is therefore a property of the algorithm rather
than of the implementation: whoever chooses a heap inherits it.

The graphics unit's reference library shows a third pattern: it slows
down by nearly the same factor in both directions, 4.95 for sorted
against 4.92 for reverse, rather than favouring one the way the heaps
do. Since sorting does not alter the value distribution
(section~\ref{sec:meth-distributions}), this is not the
radix-adversarial digit clustering of section~\ref{sec:bg-radix} but a
positional effect: the candidates surviving the critical bucket end up
clustered at one end of the array or the other, which burdens the
library's parallel compaction step symmetrically regardless of which end
they land on.

Two further points stand out here. First, bitonic sort on the neural
processing unit \emph{is} sensitive to the distribution, unlike the
other two architectures, with a variation factor of fifty-three. This
sensitivity does not originate in the array, which executes a fixed
network, but in the heap-based merge performed by the host, which, as
shown in section~\ref{sec:npu-pipeline}, dominates the time.

Second, juxtaposing the two values of $k$ isolates the cause of the
sensitivity. Map-reduce on the graphics unit is sensitive for $k$ equal
to 8, where the sorted input slows it down by a factor of 4.0, but
becomes nearly unaffected for $k$ equal to 131072, where the same input
changes it by only 4\%. The same code, with the same data, changes
behaviour because the limiting factor changes: in the second case it is
not rejection that limits it but the lack of parallelism described in
section~\ref{sec:gpu-mapreduce-limit}. When the limiting factor is not
the data, their distribution stops mattering.
