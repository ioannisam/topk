\chapter{Conclusions and Future Work}
\label{ch:conclusions}

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:concl-summary}

This thesis implemented two top-k selection algorithms on three
architectures and measured them over the same parameter space, with a
common harness and common correctness checking. The conclusions that follow
do not repeat the measurements of Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation}, but rather
what generalises from them beyond the specific hardware.

\paragraph{Top-k selection is a data-traffic problem.}
None of the nine configurations measured is bound by computational
capability. In the analysis of Section~\ref{sec:eval-roofline}, every
kernel is placed on the sloped segment of the roofline, that is, in the
region where performance is determined by bandwidth rather than by the rate
of comparisons. The practical consequence is that optimisation means
reducing the volume of data moved rather than speeding up the computation.
The thesis itself confirms this negatively: bitonic sort on the graphics
processing unit clearly makes use of a larger fraction of its roofline than
the reference library, and yet still loses, because it moves seven times as
much data.

\paragraph{The appropriate algorithm is determined by the ratio $n/k$, not
by the architecture.} The overturn in Section~\ref{sec:eval-time-k}
appears across all three architectures. The fastest implementation for k
equal to 8 is the slowest for k equal to 131072, a slowdown by a factor of
2800, while the sorting network on the same device is penalised by only a
factor of six. The cause is structural rather than implementation-specific:
the heap only begins to filter once it is full, so the available
parallelism is capped by the ratio $n/k$. The fact that independent work on
different hardware arrives at the same distinction and addresses it with
adaptive algorithm switching~\cite{lin2026adaptivetopk} reinforces the
conclusion that this is a property of the algorithms.

\paragraph{The measurement boundary determines which architecture
"wins".} In the algorithmic scope the graphics processing unit is faster
across the entire range of sizes; in the end-to-end scope the processor is
faster across that same entire range. The ranking, that is, does not
overturn somewhere in the middle; the only difference between
the two answers is where the measurement boundary is placed. This finding
has methodological significance beyond the specific problem: any speed-up
claim for offloading to an accelerator that reports only kernel time is not
verifiable, because it conceals exactly the cost that determines whether
offloading is worthwhile.

\paragraph{Low power does not imply low energy.} The neural processing unit
has the lowest power draw of any implementation, \qty{7.9}{\watt} against
\qty{15.6}{\watt} for the processor and \qty{18.6}{\watt} for the graphics
processing unit, and at the same time consumes over four times more energy than
the processor. The forty-nine-percent advantage in power is wiped out by the
eightfold disadvantage in duration. Moreover, as shown in
Section~\ref{sec:eval-energy}, there is no substantial trade-off between
time and energy for this problem: power varies among the implementations by
only a factor of 2.3, while time varies by four orders of magnitude, so the
product follows time. Of the 209 measured cases, only two belong to the
Pareto front, and both run on the processor.

Overall, for top-k selection with small k, offloading to an accelerator
does not merely fail to deliver a benefit; it is measurably worse than not
offloading at all, with the energy-delay product trailing by a factor of
between twenty-eight and seventy-eight relative to map-reduce on the
processor. The conclusion does not concern the quality of the accelerators
but the match between workload and architecture. The design principle of
neural processing units, as stated in Section~\ref{sec:bg-arch-npu}, is to
maximise local reuse of data so as to amortise the energy cost of moving
it. Top-k selection offers no reuse to exploit, so this design assumption
simply does not hold. The measurement in Section~\ref{sec:npu-pipeline},
where 61\% of the time is spent preparing and sending data and only 7\% on
waiting for the device, is the quantitative expression of this mismatch.

Finally, the sole exception deserves comment. For half-precision numbers,
bitonic sort on the neural processing unit outperforms its reference
implementation in twenty-nine measured cases. The analysis in Section~\ref{sec:eval-dtypes}
shows, however, that this advantage arises because the library handles this
particular type poorly, not because the device handles it well. This
example is instructive methodologically: a speed-up over a baseline
substantiates a claim only once it is established which of the two terms
of the ratio caused it.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:concl-limitations}

The conclusions above hold within specific limits, which are stated here
collectively.

The most significant asymmetry concerns the processor's baseline.
std::partial\_sort is strictly single-threaded, whereas this thesis's
implementations use up to eight threads, so every ratio of times between
them conflates two causes, the difference in algorithm and the difference
in core count, as already noted in
Section~\ref{sec:cpu-reference}. A thread-for-thread comparison would be
stricter and would yield smaller speed-ups. This choice is justified by the
question posed by the thesis, which concerns comparison with what the
programmer already has available, but the reader should bear it in mind.

The measured data traffic of the reference implementations is a lower
bound rather than an actual measurement, since only the input and output
are counted while the internal passes of the libraries are not observable.
Every roofline utilisation percentage reported for them in
Section~\ref{sec:eval-bandwidth} is consequently an underestimate and is
not directly comparable with the corresponding percentages of this
thesis's two implementations.

For the neural processing unit there is no on-device baseline, because no
top-k selection library exists for that architecture. The reference
implementation runs on the host, so the relevant comparisons answer the question "is
offloading worthwhile" rather than the question "how good is the
implementation relative to the best possible one on the device".

The phase-breakdown table in Section~\ref{sec:npu-pipeline} comes from a
manual run with separate instrumentation, not from the sweep that produces
the rest of the results. It is therefore not reproduced by the procedure in
Appendix~\ref{app:reproducibility} and should be read as an indicative
breakdown of time rather than as a measurement of equivalent rigour to the
others.

Finally, all measurements come from a single machine, with five data
types, four values of k, and input sizes up to $2^{24}$ elements. The
qualitative conclusions, in particular the dependence on the ratio $n/k$
and the role of the measurement boundary, are expected to carry over to
comparable systems; the specific numerical factors, however, should not be
assumed to transfer.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:concl-future}

The clearest direction follows directly from the limiting factor
identified for the neural processing unit. Since most of the time is spent
preparing and sending data per call, a workload that executes many top-k
queries at once would amortise exactly this cost; batched selection,
moreover, arises naturally in the applications mentioned in
Section~\ref{sec:bg-problem}, where selection is repeated at every step or
for every query. This extension would determine whether the unit falls
behind because of its architecture or because of the way it is fed.

In the same direction, reducing the host's involvement in pipelining, with
the device accessing data directly instead of explicit copying per chunk,
would attack the same cost from the other side. This possibility depends on
the tools and interfaces exposed by the platform and is the most
technically demanding of the open questions.

A second direction is adaptive algorithm selection. Given that the
overturn in Section~\ref{sec:eval-time-k} is systematic and predictable
from the ratio $n/k$, an implementation that dynamically chooses between
the heap and the sorting network would cover the entire parameter range
without a point of collapse. The transition point can be identified from
this thesis's own measurements.

A third direction is extending the parameter space toward points this
thesis touched on but did not exhaust: values of k larger than those
examined, where selection approaches sorting, and inputs that do not fit in
a single device's memory, where managing the stream becomes part of the
algorithm rather than of the infrastructure.

Finally, the measurement harness developed here is independent of the
specific problem. The separation of the two measurement scopes, the
recording of energy with common semantics across three architectures, and
the placement of kernels against a measured roofline are tools that can be
applied to any workload being considered for offloading. Reusing them on a
problem with substantial data reuse would provide the natural counterpart
to the results presented here.
