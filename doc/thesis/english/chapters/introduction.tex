\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:intro}

\section{Scope of the Thesis}
\label{sec:intro-scope}

This thesis examines the acceleration of top-k selection, i.e., identifying
the k largest elements of a set of n values, on three different
architectures: a general-purpose processor, a graphics processing unit, and
a neural processing unit integrated into the same package as the processor.

For each architecture, two algorithms with fundamentally different behaviour
are implemented: truncated bitonic sort, which executes a fixed sequence of
comparisons independent of the data, and map-reduce selection with local
heaps, which adapts its work to the input values. All six implementations
are measured over the same parameter space, with a common harness, common
correctness checking, and common measurement semantics, so that the
differences that emerge can be attributed to the algorithms and the
hardware rather than to the way they are measured.

The question posed is not simply which architecture is faster. It is
whether, and under what conditions, offloading the computation to an
accelerator is worthwhile, once the full cost of the offload is accounted
for and not just the kernel time. As will be shown, the two formulations of
the question give opposite answers.

\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:intro-motivation}

Neural processing units have only recently ceased to be data-centre
equipment. They are now integrated on the same die as processors in
consumer laptops, share the same main memory, and are available to any
application that could make use of them. The question "is it worth
offloading this computation to the unit?" has thus shifted from
infrastructure design to the programmer's everyday practice.

The answer is not obvious, because these units were designed for a specific
purpose. Their software ecosystem is organised around neural network
operators, and their architecture rests on the assumption that every piece
of data transferred will be used many times over. What happens when the
workload does not have this shape remains largely unexplored.

Top-k selection is a suitable vehicle for posing this question. It is a
problem simple to state but widespread in practice, appearing in
nearest-neighbour search, in retrieval and recommendation systems, and in
the sampling of language models. At the same time, it sits at the opposite
end of the spectrum from the workloads accelerators were designed for: it
performs minimal operations per element and reuses almost nothing. If
offloading pays off even here, it will pay off almost everywhere; if it
does not, we have a clear indication of the limits of usefulness of these
units.

A second, independent motivation is methodological. Accelerator comparisons
usually report kernel execution time, concealing the cost of transferring
data to and from the device. Yet this cost is exactly what determines
whether offloading is worthwhile. This thesis systematically measures both,
and shows that the choice of measurement boundary does not merely change
the numbers, but completely reverses the ranking of the architectures.

\section{Contribution}
\label{sec:intro-contribution}

The contribution of this thesis is fourfold.

\paragraph{Implementations.} Six implementations were built, two algorithms
across three architectures, with support for five data types. The processor
implementation makes use of AVX-512 vector extensions and multiple threads,
the graphics processing unit implementation uses an organisation into tiles
with shared memory, while the neural processing unit implementation was
built entirely at the level of the MLIR-AIE framework, as no top-k
selection library exists for that architecture. The correctness of every
combination was verified on 2700 cases, all successful.

\paragraph{Measurement harness.} A harness was developed that measures
every case over two explicitly distinct scopes, the algorithmic and the
end-to-end. It records energy with common semantics across all three
architectures, tallies the volume of data moved, and places every run
against a roofline measured on the same machine. The harness is independent
of the specific problem and applicable to any workload being considered for
offloading to an accelerator.

\paragraph{Findings.} The experimental evaluation yields four conclusions.
Top-k selection is a data-traffic problem on every architecture examined,
and no implementation is bound by computational throughput. The ranking of
the two algorithms inverts with the ratio $n/k$ rather than with the
architecture, with a difference reaching three orders of magnitude. The
choice of measurement boundary completely reverses the ranking of the
architectures. Finally, low power draw does not imply low energy
consumption: the neural processing unit has the lowest power of all
implementations and at the same time consumes several times more energy
(because it takes several times longer).

\paragraph{Reproducibility.} The entire experimental procedure, from
building the executables to producing the plots, is carried out by scripts
included in the repository. The measurement conditions are explicitly
stabilised and recorded together with the results. Details are given in
Appendix~\ref{app:reproducibility}.

\section{Structure of the Thesis}
\label{sec:intro-outline}

Chapter~\ref{ch:background} defines the problem, presents the families of
algorithms that solve it, and describes the execution models of the three
architectures, concluding with the existing implementations and the gap
this thesis addresses.

Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology} describes the reference system and the
experimental setup: exactly what is measured, under what semantics, with
what comparison metrics, and under what conditions. This chapter is a
prerequisite for reading the results.

Chapters~\ref{ch:cpu},~\ref{ch:gpu} and~\ref{ch:npu} present the
implementations on each of the three architectures. They follow a common
structure, so that the corresponding sections can be compared directly:
design choices, the two algorithms, memory management and data traffic,
ground truth, and finally, the limiting factors identified.

Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation} presents and interprets the results:
correctness, execution time, comparison of architectures, analysis with the
roofline model, energy evaluation, and finally, the effect of data type and
input distribution.

Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusions} summarises what generalises, states the limits
within which it holds, and proposes directions for future work.

The appendices include a list of acronyms and instructions for reproducing
the measurements.
