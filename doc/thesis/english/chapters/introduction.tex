\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:intro}

\section{Scope of the work}
\label{sec:intro-scope}

This work examines the acceleration of top-k selection, that is, finding
the k largest elements of a set of n values, on three architectures: a
general-purpose processor, a graphics unit, and a neural processing unit
integrated into the same die as the processor.

For each architecture, two algorithms with fundamentally different
behaviour are implemented. Truncated bitonic sort executes a fixed
sequence of comparisons, independent of the data; selection with local
heaps under the map-reduce scheme adapts its work to the input values.
The six implementations are measured over the same parameter space, with
a common harness and common measurement semantics, so that whatever
differences emerge are owed to the algorithms and the hardware and not to
the way of measuring.

The central question is under what conditions offloading to an
accelerator pays off, weighing both the speed of the architecture and the
additional cost of the process.

\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:intro-motivation}

Neural processing units are now integrated on the same die as the
processor of modern laptops, share its main memory, and are available to
any application that could make use of them. Whether it is worth
offloading this computation to the accelerator has become a timely
question for developing software on such hardware.

The answer is not obvious. These units were designed for a specific
workload: their software is organised around neural-network operators, and
their architecture rests on the assumption that every piece of data
transferred will be used many times. What happens when the workload does
not have this shape remains largely unexplored.

Top-k selection is a suitable vehicle for posing this question: a problem
simple to state, widespread in nearest-neighbour search, retrieval and
recommendation systems, the sampling of language models, and many other
applications. Yet it sits at the opposite end of the spectrum from the
workloads these accelerators were designed for: it performs minimal
operations per element and reuses nothing.

\section{Contribution}
\label{sec:intro-contribution}

\paragraph{Implementations.} Six implementations were built, two
algorithms across three architectures, with support for five data types.
The processor implementation exploits AVX-512 vector extensions and
multiple threads; the graphics unit implementation is organised into
tiles with shared memory; the neural processing unit implementation
was written entirely at the level of the MLIR-AIE framework. The
correctness of every combination was verified over 2700 cases.

\paragraph{Measurement harness.} A harness was developed that measures
each case in two explicitly distinct scopes, algorithmic and end-to-end.
It records energy with common semantics across the three architectures,
measures the volume of data moved, and places each run against a
roofline ceiling measured on the same machine. It is not built
specifically for the top-k problem: it applies to any workload being
considered for offloading.

\paragraph{Findings.} Top-k selection is a data-traffic problem on every
architecture examined; no implementation is limited by computational
capability. The ranking of the two algorithms depends on the ratio $n/k$ and
stays the same across all architectures. The measurement scope completely
overturns the ranking of the architectures. Energy-wise, the neural processing
unit draws the lowest power of all implementations, and yet ends up with a
particularly high energy footprint, simply because it needs far more time.

\paragraph{Reproducibility.} The entire experimental procedure runs from
scripts included in the repository. Measurement conditions are recorded
alongside the results. Details are given in
appendix~\ref{app:reproducibility}.

\section{Structure of the work}
\label{sec:intro-outline}

Chapter~\ref{ch:background} defines the problem, presents the algorithm
families that solve it, and describes the execution models of the three
architectures, concluding with the gap this work is called to fill.

Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology} presents what is measured, with what
semantics, against which baselines, and under what conditions, a
prerequisite for reading the results of Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation}.

Chapters~\ref{ch:cpu},~\ref{ch:gpu}, and~\ref{ch:npu} present the
implementations on each of the three architectures. They follow a common
structure, so that corresponding sections can be compared directly:
design choices, the two algorithms, memory management, the reference
implementation, and finally the limiting factors identified.

Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation} presents and interprets the results:
execution time, comparison across architectures, memory footprint, and
energy consumption, across different parameters, data types, and input
distributions.

Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusions} summarises what generalises, states the
limits within which it holds, and proposes directions for continuation.

The appendices include a list of acronyms, a glossary of the Greek terms
with their English equivalents, and instructions for reproducing the
measurements.
