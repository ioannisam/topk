\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:intro}

\section{Scope of the Thesis}
\label{sec:intro-scope}

This work examines the acceleration of top-k selection, that is, finding
the k largest elements of a set of n values, on three different
architectures: a general-purpose processor, a graphics unit, and a neural
processing unit integrated into the same die as the processor.

For each architecture, two algorithms with fundamentally different
behaviour are implemented: truncated bitonic sort, which executes a fixed
sequence of comparisons independent of the data, and selection with local
heaps under the map-reduce scheme, which adapts its work to the input
values. All six implementations are measured over the same parameter
space, with a common harness, common correctness checking, and common
measurement semantics, so that the differences that emerge are attributed
to the algorithms and the hardware and not to the way of measuring.

The question posed is not simply which architecture is faster, but whether
and under what conditions it pays to offload the computation to an
accelerator, once the entire cost of the offload is counted rather than
just the kernel's time. As will become clear, the two formulations of the
question give opposite answers.

\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:intro-motivation}

Neural processing units have recently stopped being data-centre equipment:
they are now integrated on the same die as the processor of consumer
laptops, share the same main memory, and are available to any application
that could make use of them. The question "is it worth offloading this
computation to the unit?" has thus shifted from infrastructure design to
the programmer's everyday practice.

The answer is not obvious, because these units were designed for a
specific purpose: their software ecosystem is organised around
neural-network operators, and their architecture rests on the assumption
that every piece of data transferred will be used many times. What happens
when the workload does not have this shape remains largely unexplored.

Top-k selection is a suitable vehicle for posing this question. It is a
problem simple to state but widespread in practice, appearing in
nearest-neighbour search, retrieval and recommendation systems, and the
sampling of language models. At the same time it sits at the opposite end
of the spectrum from the workloads accelerators were designed for: it
performs minimal operations per element and reuses almost nothing. If
offloading pays off even here, it will pay off almost everywhere; if it
does not, that is a clear indication of the limits of these units'
usefulness.

A second, independent motivation is methodological. Accelerator
comparisons usually report the kernel's execution time, hiding the cost of
transferring data to and from the device. This is precisely the cost that
determines whether offloading pays off. This work systematically measures
both, and shows that the choice of measurement boundary does not merely
change the numbers, but completely overturns the ranking of the
architectures.

\section{Contribution}
\label{sec:intro-contribution}

The contribution of this work is fourfold.

\paragraph{Implementations.} Six implementations were built, two algorithms
across three architectures, with support for five data types. The
processor implementation exploits AVX-512 vector extensions and multiple
threads, the GPU implementation uses tile-based organisation with shared
memory, and the NPU implementation was built entirely at the level of the
MLIR-AIE framework. The correctness of every combination was verified over 2700
cases, all successful.

\paragraph{Measurement harness.} A harness was developed that measures each
case in two explicitly distinct scopes, algorithmic and end-to-end. It
records energy with common semantics across all three architectures,
counts the volume of data moved, and places each run against a ceiling
measured on the same machine. The harness is independent of the specific
problem and applicable to any workload being considered for offloading.

\paragraph{Findings.} The experimental evaluation yields four conclusions.
Top-k selection is a data-traffic problem on every architecture examined,
and no implementation is limited by computational capability. The ranking
of the two algorithms is overturned by the ratio $n/k$ rather than by the
architecture, by a margin reaching three orders of magnitude. The choice
of measurement boundary completely overturns the ranking of the
architectures. Finally, low power draw does not imply low energy
consumption: the neural processing unit has the lowest power of all
implementations and simultaneously consumes several times more energy,
because it needs several times more time.

\paragraph{Reproducibility.} The entire experimental procedure, from
building the executables to producing the plots, runs from scripts
included in the repository. Measurement conditions are explicitly pinned
and recorded alongside the results. Details are given in
appendix~\ref{app:reproducibility}.

\section{Structure of the Thesis}
\label{sec:intro-outline}

Chapter~\ref{ch:background} defines the problem, presents the algorithm
families that solve it, and describes the execution models of the three
architectures, concluding with existing implementations and the gap this
work is called to fill.

Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology} describes the reference system and
experimental setup: exactly what is measured, with what semantics, against
which baselines, and under what conditions, a prerequisite for reading
the results.

Chapters~\ref{ch:cpu},~\ref{ch:gpu}, and~\ref{ch:npu} present the
implementations on each of the three architectures. They follow a common
structure, so that corresponding sections can be compared directly: design
choices, the two algorithms, memory management and data traffic, the
reference implementation, and finally the limiting factors identified.

Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation} presents and interprets the results:
correctness, execution time, comparison across architectures, roofline
analysis, energy assessment, and finally the effect of data type and input
distribution.

Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusions} summarises what generalises, states the
limits within which it holds, and proposes directions for continuation.

The appendices include a list of acronyms and instructions for reproducing
the measurements.
